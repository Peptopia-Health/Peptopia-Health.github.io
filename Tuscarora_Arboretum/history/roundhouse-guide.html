Tuscarora Arboretum · History Guide No. 2

The Roundhouse & the Great Strike

Where America’s First National Strike Began

Martinsburg, West Virginia · November 2026

The Story, in Brief

On the banks of Tuscarora Creek stands the oldest fully covered roundhouse in the United States — the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad’s West Roundhouse of 1866, a sixteen-sided brick dome carried on a cast-iron frame designed by Albert Fink. The shops around it were the B&O’s central repair point between Baltimore and Cumberland, and on July 16, 1877, the men who worked here started the Great Railroad Strike — America’s first nationwide labor uprising. Within days 100,000 men were out, from Baltimore to San Francisco. Burned in one war and rebuilt, then struck in another kind of war, the complex survives today as a National Historic Landmark — the most intact strike site in the nation.

🚂 1 · The Railroad Reaches Martinsburg ↑ Timeline

The Baltimore and Ohio Railroad — chartered in 1827, the first public-use railroad in the United States — pushed west from Baltimore and reached Martinsburg on May 21, 1842. The town sat at the natural midpoint of the run to Cumberland, and in 1848 the railroad began expanding its facilities here, making Martinsburg the central maintenance area between Baltimore and Cumberland. Construction began with a rectangular engine shed that year; more acreage followed in 1852, and with it a machine shop for heavier repairs. A railroad town was born along Tuscarora Creek.

🔥 2 · War on the B&O ↑ Timeline

Martinsburg’s railroad straddled the boundary between North and South, and when the Civil War came, the B&O became a strategic prize both armies fought over. In mid-1861, Confederate troops under Stonewall Jackson destroyed the original Martinsburg shops, the roundhouse, and the depot. The line was raided and torn up again and again before the war ended — the town changed hands dozens of times, and its railroad plant was left in ruins.

🏭 3 · The West Roundhouse Rises — 1866 ↑ Timeline

The B&O rebuilt in 1866, raising three buildings that still stand: the West Roundhouse, the Machine and Woodworking Shop, and the Car Shop. The roundhouse was designed by Albert Fink, the renowned civil engineer and railroad economist: a sixteen-sided polygon under a domed roof, the roof carried on an early cast-iron frame whose intricate pattern, seen from inside, resembles a spider’s web. Around the central 50-foot turntable, sixteen locomotive bays opened across more than 20,000 square feet of floor — crews inspected, oiled, and fueled each engine at the end of its run, then swung it on the turntable toward its next assignment. The two rectangular shops were designed by architect Johann Niernsee. Built in 1866, the West Roundhouse is the oldest fully covered roundhouse in the United States. A twin, the East Roundhouse, followed in 1872.

📉 4 · Hard Times & the Wage Cuts ↑ Timeline

The Panic of 1873 plunged the country into a long depression, and by 1877 working Americans were at the lowest ebb of it. The great railroads answered hard times by cutting wages again and again. In July 1877 the four major trunk lines — the Baltimore & Ohio, the Pennsylvania, the Erie, and the New York Central — announced another 10 percent wage cut. For B&O men it was the second cut in eight months, and it was set to take effect on July 16. The frustration that had been building for four years reached its explosive point — here.

✊ 5 · July 16, 1877 — The Strike Ignites ↑ Timeline

On the morning of July 16, 1877, after a first, aborted stoppage by a handful of B&O men in Baltimore, the workers at the Martinsburg shops stopped work. They uncoupled the locomotives, confined them in the roundhouse, and declared that no trains would leave Martinsburg unless the cut was rescinded. As the strikers demonstrated at the depot, Governor Henry M. Mathews ordered in the closest state militia — Colonel Charles Faulkner’s command — but the blockade held. On the morning of July 17, the militia fatally wounded William Vandergriff, a striking fireman, as he tried to stop a train from leaving the station — the first blood of the Great Strike, shed outside these shops. The governor appealed to Washington, and President Hayes sent federal troops: Harper’s Weekly, which put the blockade on its cover, counted 1,500 freight cars and 13 locomotives standing blocked in and about the town when the regulars arrived on July 19.

🌎 6 · The Fire Spreads ↑ Timeline

Spontaneously, and without united leadership, the strike leapt from railroad town to railroad town — to a dozen centers including Baltimore, where fighting erupted on July 20, Pittsburgh, where the roundhouse and Union Depot burned on July 21–22, and on to Chicago, St. Louis, and San Francisco. Within a few days 100,000 men were on strike in the first nationwide labor upheaval in the history of the United States. Federal troops reopened the B&O step by step: a freight train rolled out of Cumberland under escort on July 27 and reached Martinsburg without incident, and the main line was fully reopened by July 31. The verdict of the day gave the advantage to the workers: the Great Strike put a stop to wage-cutting — on the railroads and elsewhere — and in August the B&O began granting home-visit leave and assurances of full-time work. Martinsburg’s shops, remarkably, escaped the destruction visited on so many other railroad centers.

🏛️ 7 · The Roundhouse Today ↑ Timeline

The complex served the railroad long after 1877 — as a locomotive service facility until about 1899, then in bridge and switch fabrication and car repairs. CSX, the B&O’s modern successor, closed the shops in 1985; the East Roundhouse was lost to fire in 1990. In 1999 the Berkeley County Commission bought the 12.94-acre complex from CSX for $150,000, and in 2000 it passed to the Berkeley County Roundhouse Authority, which has restored and redeveloped the site. In 2003 the B&O Railroad Martinsburg Shops became West Virginia’s 16th National Historic Landmark, and the West Roundhouse is also a National Historic Civil Engineering Landmark. Today the roundhouse hosts tours, public events, and private gatherings. No other site where strike activity occurred remains as intact — the Great Strike began at this very location, and the building where it began still stands on the banks of Tuscarora Creek.
